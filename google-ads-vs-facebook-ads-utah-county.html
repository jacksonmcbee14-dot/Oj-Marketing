Google Ads vs. Facebook Ads: which should you run first?

A Facebook lead costs about $27 on average. A Google lead costs about $67. That doesn't mean Facebook wins. Here's how a Utah County business should actually choose, with the 2026 numbers.

By Jackson McBee, co-founder · November 20, 2026 · 7 min read
The short answer

Start with Google if people already search for what you sell (plumbers, dentists, auto repair, lawyers). They're looking for you right now, and Google puts you in front of them.

Start with Facebook and Instagram if people don't search for it yet (a new café, a boutique, a gym, an event). You have to show it to them before they know they want it.

Most local businesses end up running both. The question is which one gets your first dollar.

The one difference that matters: searching vs. scrolling

Every comparison article lists twenty differences. Only one really decides it.

Google Ads catch demand. Someone types "water heater repair Orem" or "dentist near me." They already have the problem and they want someone to fix it today. Your ad shows up at the exact moment they're ready to call.

Facebook and Instagram ads create demand. Nobody opens Instagram looking for a new brunch spot. They're scrolling, your ad shows up, and it gives them a reason to want something they weren't thinking about five seconds ago. You pick who sees it by location, age and interests, like "people within 10 miles of Provo who follow fitness accounts."

So the real question isn't "which platform is better." It's: are people already searching for what I sell?

Put $1,000 a month into each at those averages and the math looks lopsided: about 15 leads from Google and about 36 from Facebook.

But a "lead" isn't the same thing on both. A Google lead usually searched for your exact service and then called or filled out your form. A Facebook lead often tapped a pre-filled form while scrolling past. Facebook gives you more names; Google tends to give you people who are closer to buying. Cost per customer is the number that matters, and you only learn it by tracking which leads actually pay you.

Two things jump out:

  • Beauty and personal care is cheaper on Google. Salons, lash studios and med spas get leads for about $39 on Google versus about $51 on Facebook. People search "lash extensions Lehi" with their wallet out.
  • Dentists pay almost the same on both. When the price is close, go where the intent is. Someone searching "emergency dentist Provo" is worth more than someone who tapped an ad between memes.

And some businesses barely get searched at all. If you just opened a new dessert shop in Vineyard, Google has almost nobody to show your ad to yet. Facebook and Instagram are how people find out you exist.

Three questions to decide where your first dollar goes

1. Do people search for what you sell?

Type your service and your city into Google, like "roof repair Springville." If you see ads and a map with competitors, people are searching and others are paying to catch them. That's a Google signal. If the results are thin, lean Facebook.

2. Is it urgent or impulse?

Urgent problems (a leak, a toothache, a broken car) get searched. Impulse buys (a new boutique, a fun class, a weekend event) get discovered while scrolling. Urgent leans Google. Impulse leans Facebook and Instagram.

3. Does it look good on camera?

Facebook and Instagram are visual. Food, fitness, home makeovers, clothes and anything with a great before-and-after does well there. If your work photographs well, Meta ads have something to work with. If it's hard to show (accounting, insurance), Google's text ads are often the better start.

Rule of thumb: two or three answers pointing the same way means start there. A split answer usually means run both with a small test budget on each, and move money toward whichever brings in paying customers, not just the cheapest leads.

Mistakes that waste the budget on either platform

  • No conversion tracking. If you can't see which calls and forms came from an ad, you can't tell what's working. WordStream looked at more than 15,000 Google Ads accounts and found nearly 29% recorded zero conversions over 90 days.
  • Skipping negative keywords on Google. Without them, a plumber can pay for clicks on "plumber jobs" or "how to fix a toilet yourself." The same WordStream analysis found accounts using negative keywords had conversion rates up to three times higher.
  • Targeting too wide on Facebook. A Pleasant Grove salon doesn't need people in Salt Lake City seeing its ads. Keep the radius tight to where customers will actually drive.
  • Judging it in week one. Both platforms need a few weeks of data before the numbers settle. Test, adjust weekly, and judge on the month.
  • Sending ad clicks to your homepage. Send people to a page about the exact thing the ad promised, with your phone number and a form right there.

Quick questions

How much should a small business spend to start?

Enough to get real data. As a rough guide, at the average Google cost per click of $5.42, a $300 test buys about 55 clicks. That's enough to see if anyone converts. Less than that and you're mostly guessing.

Do Instagram ads count as Facebook ads?

Yes. Both run through Meta's ad system, and one campaign can show on Facebook, Instagram or both. That's why you'll hear them called "Meta ads."

Is one platform faster?

Both can start showing ads the day a campaign is approved. Google tends to bring calls faster for urgent services because people are already looking. Facebook usually needs a little longer to find the right audience.

Should I run ads myself or hire someone?

If you have a few hours a week and enjoy the numbers, you can absolutely learn it. If you'd rather run your business, an agency handles setup, weekly testing and tracking. Either way, the first step is the same: figure out which platform fits your business using the three questions above.
